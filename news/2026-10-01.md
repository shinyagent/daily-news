## AI NEWS

**1. Google releases Gemini 4 Argon — but only "trusted cyber defenders" can use it for now**
Summary: Google announced Gemini 4 Argon, its new frontier model, delivering frontier performance in software engineering, enterprise knowledge work like legal and finance, and cybersecurity defense, and says it is already powering internal Google workflows with thousands of Googlers using it. Rather than a general launch, Argon is rolling out only to a set of vetted "trusted cyber defenders" through Google's Fairwind Program, a phased approach Google says is required to release frontier capabilities this powerful safely and which is timed to the U.S. government's voluntary pre-release model access process. Introductory pricing is $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens with cached input at 95% off, and Ars Technica notes the launch effectively kills the expected Gemini 3.5 Pro; it drew 860 points and 575 comments on Hacker News.

**2. Six AI giants sign Trump's "morally binding" self-regulation accord**
Summary: The White House published the full text of the Joint Commitment on Frontier Responsibilities, a voluntary AI safety accord signed by Google's Sundar Pichai, Anthropic's Dario Amodei, Meta's Mark Zuckerberg, OpenAI's Greg Brockman, xAI's Elon Musk and Nvidia's Jensen Huang — plus Trump himself, whose title is misspelled "President of the Unites States" in the document. It commits companies to four things: robust internal controls monitoring model capabilities and alignment during training and deployment across cyber, bio and chemical threats and preventing models from accessing systems in unintended ways; an empowered internal team to do that work and remediate failures; an external monitor empowered to run independent assessments; and a board committee to receive reports on all of it, with the firms meeting regularly to set standards. Wired calls it a "fancy pinky-swear" with no enforcement teeth, and the same week the FTC signalled it intends to investigate several AI companies over consumer protection, so a blanket government blessing did not materialize.
Source: https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/1002584/trump-us-ai-safety-deal-self-regulation-tech-execs | https://www.wired.com/story/trumps-ai-safety-accord-is-a-fancy-pinky-swear/

**3. Reddit is killing RSS feeds and its public API to fight AI scraping**
Summary: Reddit told moderators and developers it is ending RSS support on November 13 and shutting down its public API in March 2027, describing RSS as having become "a common surface for large-scale scraping and automated abuse." The move continues Reddit's years-long tightening of access to its user-generated content, which has become a prized training and grounding corpus for AI systems — earlier this year it was designated a very large online platform under the EU's Digital Services Act. It also lands as AI crawlers and agents put unprecedented load on open web infrastructure, and removes one of the last low-friction ways independent readers and small apps could follow the site.
Source: https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/30/reddit-is-killing-rss-feeds-ending-public-api-access-because-of-ai-bots/

**4. Google's pilot paying publishers for AI search contributions is live — and the money is tiny**
Summary: Google is running a pilot that pays roughly 100 publishers for how much their content contributed to AI Overviews, AI Mode and the Gemini chatbot, with Digiday reporting the program started less than a year ago; The Information found one early participant earned over $1 million in a year while a more recent joiner has made only about $50,000 to $60,000. Ars Technica adds that many participating sites see just one-tenth of one percent of their advertising revenue coming from the AI payments. The pilot is Google's attempt to head off publisher anger over traffic collapse from AI answers, but the amounts suggest it is nowhere near a substitute for the referral traffic those features displaced.

**9. OpenAI clones TypeSafe's Jev with a "Decisions API" aimed at keeping swarming agents in check**
Summary: In a DevDay aside, Sam Altman revealed OpenAI's new Decisions API, which lets developers hand a model like Luna a predefined set of options — image categories, or discrete agent behaviors — to choose between, producing cheap, extremely fast probabilistic output while retaining image understanding, language support and safety protections. It is functionally the same shape as Jev, the "System One" decision model TypeSafe AI released this month, and TypeSafe CEO Diogo Almeida, a former OpenAI engineer, joked on X about the beginning of the "clone wars." The framing matters because fast, cheap classifiers are how labs expect to police fleets of agents — the same containment problem Nvidia addressed last week with its Open Agent Safety Platform.
Source: https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/30/openais-jev-clone-could-help-the-frontier-lab-stop-its-swarming-agents/

**10. Google can now watermark AI-designed proteins to catch them in biosecurity screening**
Summary: Ars Technica reports Google has worked out how to embed detectable watermarks in proteins produced by AI design tools, so existing DNA-synthesis screening software can flag them — a gap that mattered because biosecurity screening was built to catch sequences of natural or malicious origin, not novel AI-generated designs. AI protein design has already produced enzymes that digest plastic and proteins that block venom, but the same tools could be turned to toxins or modified viral proteins. It is one of the few areas where biosecurity defenders appear to be moving ahead of a threat rather than reacting after it.
Source: https://arstechnica.com/science/2026/09/google-figures-out-how-to-watermark-ai-designed-proteins/

## TRAVEL TECH NEWS

**1. Revolut's travel distribution play faces its U.S. test**
Summary: Revolut, the London-based digital bank, has turned travel payments into a distribution business in Europe and is now preparing to bring the same strategy to the United States on the back of conditional approval for a U.S. banking license. Its travel offering has grown from 2015 currency-exchange cards for tourists into hotel stays, experiences, trip insurance, lounge access, points convertible into airline miles and Revolut Pay wallet checkout. The bet is that keeping travel spend inside a financial ecosystem — rather than handing it to an OTA — is the durable wedge, and the U.S. market is where that thesis gets tested at scale.
Source: https://skift.com/2026/09/30/revoluts-travel-distribution-play-faces-a-u-s-test/

**2. Choice Hotels buys RV club Harvest Hosts for $130 million**
Summary: Choice Hotels agreed to buy Harvest Hosts, a membership club for recreational-vehicle travelers with about half a million users, for roughly $130 million in cash — its first acquisition under new CEO Dom Dragisich and the franchisor's first move outside hotels. Choice is buying the company from growth-equity firm Stripes, which paid $37 million for it in 2021, and will fund the deal from cash and its revolver. The strategic question is whether an RV membership base converts into hotel room nights; if it doesn't, the deal drifts from the net-rooms-growth priority Dragisich set on taking the job.
Source: https://skift.com/2026/09/30/choice-hotels-buys-rv-club-harvest-hosts-for-130-million/

**3. Skift: the travel distribution funnel is coming apart, and "who owns the customer" is the wrong question**
Summary: Skift's analysis, drawn from Global Forum conversations with Uber's Dara Khosrowshahi, Sierra's Bret Taylor, Booking Holdings' Glenn Fogel, Expedia's Ariane Gorin and Airbnb's Brian Chesky, argues AI is fragmenting travel distribution across many starting points rather than simply replacing Google with a new gatekeeper. The old model — traveler types intent into a search box, Google sees it first, suppliers and OTAs compete for the click — depended on intent being visible early and in one place; assistants break that assumption. For travel companies the practical consequence is that discovery, merchandising and attribution all need rethinking at once, not just a better search box.
Source: https://skift.com/2026/09/30/the-travel-distribution-funnel-is-coming-apart/

**4. Airbnb launches consumer AI tools in the U.S., playing catch-up to OTA rivals**
Summary: Airbnb rolled out a set of consumer-facing AI features in the U.S., headlined by natural-language search by text or voice for homes, hotels, experiences and services on its homepage and app, plus AI filters that anticipate intent — searching "baby" surfaces cribs, playgrounds and toys as filters. Search results also carry AI-generated listing descriptions and AI property comparisons. Skift's read is that being late is not disqualifying — Kayak was not first to metasearch — but the outcome depends on whether Airbnb's tools are actually better than Booking's and Expedia's, which have shipped similar features for far longer.

**5. United plans three tiers of premium fares on domestic and short-haul routes**
Summary: United told staff it will launch three fare tiers inside its United First and United Business cabins on domestic and select short-haul international flights, extending a segmentation model it already runs on long-haul routes. Airlines have used unbundled premium tiers to sell flexibility and amenities separately from the seat, and the domestic bet is that business travelers will pay more for changeability even on shorter trips. It is one of the clearest current examples of airline retailing moving from fare buckets to productized, ancillary-driven premium offers.
