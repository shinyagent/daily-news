## AI NEWS

**1. OpenAI's chief scientist wants perplexity to be the yardstick for an AI "speed limit"**
Summary: Jakub Pachocki published "Perplexity and Intelligence" arguing that cross-entropy loss on a held-out dataset — not capability benchmarks, which saturate and go stale — is the one measure that has tracked progress consistently, and that it should become the shared metric companies use to agree on limits. The post publishes OpenAI's own internal numbers for the first time: log-perplexity of about 0.99 at GPT-3.5, 0.91 at GPT-4 and below 0.8 now, the level at which he says "transformative capabilities" in cybersecurity and mathematics started appearing. His proposal is that labs commit not to cross a threshold until monitoring reaches a concrete milestone — Sam Altman called it "the most important thing OpenAI has published in a while," and it drew 446 points on Hacker News, where the argument that perplexity is comparable across labs at all was the main fight.
Source: https://openai.com/index/perplexity-and-intelligence/

**2. Banks are banning your AI agent from your bank account — and putting the losses on you**
Summary: JPMorgan Chase changed its terms in late October to bar third-party agents that log in with a customer's credentials, Bank of America warned customers the same day not to hand over login details, and Wells Fargo, Citibank and PNC have since added agent clauses that make bot-caused losses the customer's problem. The pushback is spreading beyond finance: Amazon blocked Meta's Muse from making purchases, and United now blocks personal bots from booking flights on a user's behalf. Analysts frame it as institutions getting ahead of a fad — Forrester's Sucharita Kodali says most people "don't even know what agents are" — but the policy question it settles is real: consumers authorize goals, not every click.
Source: https://www.wired.com/story/banks-are-cracking-down-on-ai-agents/

**3. Nvidia stops selling H200 chips in China as it reports soaring revenue**
Summary: Interim CEO Colette Kress said Nvidia will no longer sell its H200 accelerators in China, a step that lines up with the Trump administration's move to close the remote-access loophole that let Chinese firms rent capacity in Malaysia and elsewhere. It came in the same week the company reported surging revenue and insisted it has no plans to bring back Jensen Huang, even as Elon Musk and other executives circulate a petition for his return — a split TechCrunch's Equity podcast called "the tale of two Nvidias." Analysts estimate exposure to China at roughly 20% of the business through indirect channels, so the exit is a real revenue decision rather than a symbolic one.
Source: https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/21/a-tale-of-two-nvidias/

**4. Binding of Isaac studio pulls AI music after Suno metadata surfaces in the game files**
Summary: Players found that a Grand Theft Auto-parody segment of The Binding of Isaac: Repentance Plus used tracks carrying Suno metadata, and the game was review-bombed on Steam within days of the discovery. Creator Edmund McMillen apologized and said he "had no idea" generative music had been used, while Nicalis — the studio behind the release — appears not to have disclosed it anywhere, despite Valve's requirement that developers declare generative AI use. Nicalis removed the files within hours of the discovery, but the game is still rated "overwhelmingly negative" — an early test of what AI disclosure rules actually cost a small studio.
Source: https://www.theverge.com/games/1039770/binding-of-isaac-ai-music

**5. A live agent swarm is grinding at P vs NP, with Lean checking every lemma**
Summary: pequalnp.com is running a coordinated fleet of coding agents — the site invites pasting its instructions into Claude Code, Cursor, Codex or Gemini CLI — against the most famous open problem in computer science, publishing a live ledger of lemmas stated, attempts begun, and verdicts from a Lean checker. Sponsors can fund the work but explicitly not the verdict, and the site is careful that its progress display is a count of checked lemmas aimed at each answer, "not a probability" and "not a proof." It follows Axiom Math's 24-day Lean formalization of the Classification of Finite Simple Groups and, more immediately, the Riemann hypothesis claims of the past week — AI mathematics has become a public spectator sport with a scoreboard.
Source: https://pequalnp.com/

**6. Twenty years of mod_wsgi lessons went into an AI-assisted Rust server for Python**
Summary: Graham Dumpleton, who started mod_wsgi in 2006, has spent the last three weeks building Muru, a Rust server for ASGI and WSGI Python apps, and publishing an unusually detailed running account of the work — which is how Simon Willison found it. Dumpleton's own framing of the AI question is the interesting part: the model does much of the surface work, but "deciding what isn't good enough yet is still down to me," and two decades of getting production Python serving wrong is what makes those judgments possible. It is a concrete counter-example to both the "AI writes your project" and "AI is useless" camps, from someone rebuilding his own most famous tool.
Source: https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/21/muru/

## TRAVEL TECH NEWS

**1. WeRoad hands the customer relationship to Korea's NOL instead of selling direct**
Summary: Italian adventure-travel operator WeRoad is entering South Korea without selling a single trip itself, letting Yanolja-owned travel and leisure platform NOL Universe own the customer inside its app and website — the first seven itineraries go live in early December, with departures starting in 2027. Chief strategy officer Andrea Lamperini told Skift the company may "leave the South Korea market covered by the partnership with NOL and never go direct." That inverts the usual channel debate: instead of a supplier fighting an OTA for the customer, a Western operator is deliberately renting distribution from a superapp, betting local ownership beats a branded storefront in an unfamiliar market.
Source: https://skift.com/2026/11/22/italys-weroad-lets-korean-superapp-nol-own-its-customers-exclusive/

**2. Delta's weight-and-balance automation failed and the manual fallback became the story**
Summary: Delta's automated system for calculating aircraft weight and center of gravity went down Sunday morning, prompting the FAA to briefly issue a ground stop for Atlanta-bound Delta flights before downgrading it to a ground delay, while crews worked weight-and-balance figures by hand to keep departures moving. About 1,010 Delta flights — roughly 30% of the day's schedule — were delayed by late morning, with the FAA warning up to 3,500 could be affected at hubs including Atlanta and LaGuardia, in the opening weekend of Thanksgiving travel. The FAA stressed the fault was Delta's alone and not an air traffic control issue, but it landed a week after a nationwide ground stop, and made the case that a single airline back-office calculation is now a national-scale single point of failure.
Source: https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/22/us/delta-flight-delays-system-outage.html
