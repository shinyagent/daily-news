## AI NEWS

**1. OpenAI and Anthropic have cut LLM prices as much as 80% since June — and the cost of cache reads explains most of it**
Summary: Max Woolf's timeline traces the tit-for-tat: GPT-5.6 Luna down 80% to $0.20/$1.20/$0.02 per million tokens, Sonnet 5's launch price made permanent, a temporary ~20% cut to GPT-5.6 Sol, Fable 5.1 taking 75% off cache reads, then GPT-6 Sol and Luna launched at half the previous input/output price. Anthropic's counter was Opus 5.5 at $4/$20/$0.20 — a 20% input/output cut plus a larger cache cut that works out to 40-60% cheaper overall — and GPT-6.1 Sol halved its cache price again to $0.10, leaving that model family about 75% cheaper cumulatively. His explanation is commodity economics rather than generosity: with cache reads 96-99% of agent token spend and a model switchable via one toggle in a harness like OpenCode or Zed or an OpenRouter endpoint string, there is no moat left, and 29 providers now host DeepSeek's V4.1 Flash variant alone. Vercel's gateway data shows cost per token at 43.3% of April 2026 levels while aggregate spend still rises, because capability is improving faster than prices fall.
Source: https://minimaxir.com/2026/10/cheap-llms/

**2. A dermatologist found a 10-year-old CNN beats GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.1 at diagnosing skin cancer**
Summary: Dr Magnus Lynch ran 200 biopsy-proven lesions from the public MILK10k dataset — each with a clinical photo and a dermoscopic image — through both frontier models and a fine-tuned Inception v3, asking for the likeliest of six diagnoses, a confidence, and whether the lesion could be safely discharged. The CNN got 62% right; GPT-6 Astra got 42% and Claude Fable 5.1 43%. The failure mode matters more than the score: on a thin invasive melanoma the CNN and Fable 5.1 both said melanoma while GPT-6 Astra called it a basal cell carcinoma with 95% confidence, and on a benign seborrhoeic keratosis both LLMs misread it as an actinic keratosis that needs treatment. The post drew 383 points on Hacker News, and its argument is that general-purpose frontier models are being pitched into narrow, high-stakes classification tasks where a small supervised model trained for 20 minutes still wins.
Source: https://lynchlab.net/llm-skin-cancer

**3. Cerebras serves a 3-trillion-parameter Kimi K3 across wafers at 1,000-2,000+ tokens per second**
Summary: Cerebras published how it splits a frontier model over multiple WSE-3T wafers, each with 44GB of on-chip memory — nowhere near enough for a trillion-parameter weight set, so layers are partitioned by type across the wafer array. Using open-weight Kimi K3 (3 trillion parameters, 69 linear-attention layers and 24 dense layers) as the worked example, the company claims multi-trillion-parameter serving at 1,000-2,000+ tokens per second, on a model it benchmarks at 88.3% on Terminal-Bench 2.1 versus 88.8% for GPT-5.6 Sol and 91.2% on BrowseComp versus 90.4%. It drew 348 points on Hacker News, and the interesting claim is that memory bandwidth — not compute — is the binding constraint that wafer-scale integration is meant to remove.
Source: https://www.cerebras.ai/blog/how-we-deploy-trillion-parameter-frontier-models-on-cerebras

**4. A Fields medalist stopped grading homework because AI made correctness scores meaningless**
Summary: Richard Borcherds, the UC Berkeley mathematician who won the Fields Medal in 1998, told the Daily Californian he no longer has homework graded for correctness — students were using AI to score consistently at the top, so paying readers to check answers had "no point." He now grades on completion, which he calls "not a very good system" but the only alternative he has, and says that at current rates of improvement "in a few years, there'll be nothing human mathematicians can do that AI can't do better." The remarks followed OpenAI's claim that its agents had solved the Navier-Stokes existence and smoothness problem, and he added that he no longer knows what to advise students entering a field that looked AI-proof five years ago.
Source: https://www.dailycal.org/news/campus/academics/ai-use-leads-uc-berkeley-math-professor-to-see-no-point-in-grading-homework/article_bdf1eae2-c08b-491f-b9f1-81a7cea177de.html

**5. Grok shipped the first seven minutes of its AI-generated "Odyssey" — as a series, not the film Musk promised**
Summary: Three months after Elon Musk said Grok would release a full-length, "historically accurate" Odyssey, xAI published seven minutes of what it now calls "Episode 1 of our Odyssey series," made with Grok Imagine in collaboration with Wonder Studios and director Dev Walker. Musk had attacked Christopher Nolan's film version over its casting; Nolan's film, shot practically across six countries, has been an Imax box-office phenomenon since July. TechCrunch's read is that the footage is better than expected shot-to-shot but has no artistry or human interest behind it — a demonstration that generative video can now approximate cinematic texture while producing nothing a studio would release.
Source: https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/25/grok-releases-the-first-seven-minutes-of-its-ai-generated-odyssey/

**6. JetBrains open-sourced ThinkRail, an agentic IDE that keeps durable project knowledge in "living specs"**
Summary: ThinkRail wraps the pi coding agent in a desktop app and CLI, isolates each task in its own git-worktree workspace with a separate branch so agents can run in parallel without touching the developer's checkout, and gives the agent lasting context through living specs and reusable skills rather than re-reading the repo each session. It is Apache 2.0, ships with signed SHA256SUMS on the releases page, and has 1.7k stars; JetBrains says the next step is full extension support so ThinkRail can build the tools it needs based on how a team actually works. It drew 158 points on Hacker News, where the debate was whether spec-driven context is a real improvement over letting the agent rediscover structure each run.
Source: https://github.com/JetBrains/thinkrail

**7. The US fight over data centers is now an election issue — and Europe already ran the experiment**
Summary: WIRED interviewed Paris Marx, author of Hyperscale, on what the US anti-data-center movement can learn from abroad: the Netherlands and Singapore imposed moratoriums as far back as 2019, Ireland's grid became the flashpoint in 2022, and in The Dalles, Oregon, Google lobbied the city council to keep its water-use figures secret for the better part of a year before residents obtained the real numbers. Marx's point is that the pattern of secrecy followed by local backlash is well documented, and that data centers have become one of the issues that could decide key midterm races in under two weeks. The piece is a useful correction to the framing of siting fights as NIMBYism — the recurring triggers are grid capacity, water disclosure and tax abatements.
Source: https://www.wired.com/story/what-the-us-anti-data-center-movement-can-learn-from-everywhere-else/

**8. r/opensource banned AI projects — and restricted non-English posters — setting off a revolt**
Summary: The moderators of r/opensource posted new submission rules on Saturday declaring that "if your project uses AI, is made by AI, or is intended for AI, it's not welcome," arguing that AI as it exists today is "incongruent to this Open Source community," alongside restrictions on non-English speakers. The rules landed badly with a community whose entire premise is that contribution should be judged on the merits of the code and license rather than on who or what produced it, and The Verge reports the change has sparked an open rebellion among members. It is a small skirmish but a live one: the open-source world has no settled answer on whether AI-generated or AI-dependent contributions belong in it, and moderation is being used as a proxy for a licensing and provenance debate nobody has resolved.
Source: https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/1018217/reddit-opensource-ai-ban

## TRAVEL TECH NEWS

**1. Hotel booking engines are quietly becoming full commerce platforms for the whole guest journey**
Summary: A HotelTechnologyNews analysis argues the category's original constraint — a booking engine that reserves a room and nothing else — is finally breaking, because spa appointments, restaurant covers, tee times, excursions and event tickets have stayed siloed in their own systems of record, bridged only by link-outs or pre-arrival campaigns. The new generation merchandises rooms alongside ancillaries in one continuous flow, and the durable advantage is not the checkout but the forward-looking demand signal in anonymized shopping behavior, which the piece speculates could be worth a 2% channel shift a year. Its sharpest claim is a corrective to the agentic-booking hype: fully autonomous travel booking remains distant because ARI integration across legacy systems is the hard part, so the near-term opportunity is AI-assisted curation inside the booking flow rather than an agent that books end to end.
Source: https://hoteltechnologynews.com/2026/10/how-hotel-booking-engines-are-evolving-into-platforms-that-cover-the-entire-guest-journey/
